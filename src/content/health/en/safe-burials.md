## Why burials matter

The virus is present in the blood and body fluids of a person who has died of Ebola disease, and it can pass to anyone who touches them. WHO counts mourners who touch the body during funerals or burial rituals among the people most at risk, and reports that burials involving contact with the body increase the spread of the virus.

This is why, during an outbreak, funerals need to be organised differently, so that families can say goodbye without putting themselves in danger. See also [How Ebola spreads](../transmission/).

## If someone dies at home

If a person dies at home and Ebola disease is possible:

- do not touch, wash, dress or prepare the body for burial;
- do not touch the person's bedding, clothes or other objects soiled with blood, vomit or stools;
- contact the local health authorities or your national health hotline immediately.

You can find official helplines on our [helplines page](../../help/).

## What a safe and dignified burial is

Trained teams from the health authorities carry out the burial. They protect themselves, the family and the community from contact with the body. WHO asks that the burial be carried out in accordance with the family's wishes. The aim is to keep the living safe while treating the person who has died, and their family, with respect.

Talk to the team about your family's customs and wishes before the burial.

## Why it protects everyone

WHO lists safe and dignified burials among the main measures that stop Ebola outbreaks, together with care for the sick, the follow-up of contacts and the involvement of communities. Outbreaks are controlled best when communities are well informed and take part in the response.

## If you touched the body

If you touched the body or the body fluids of a person who may have died of Ebola, tell a health worker. Your health may need to be followed for 21 days. See [If you have been in contact with someone who has Ebola](../contacts/).
